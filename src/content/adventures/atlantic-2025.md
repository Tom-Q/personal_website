From September to December 2025 I travelled to Latin America, living several adventures along the way. Partly for climate reasons and partly because it seemed like an interesting thing to do, I decided to sail there rather than fly.

There are several ways to cross the Atlantic by sea. A popular one is to find a skipper doing the trip in a small sailing vessel, apply to join as crew, and pay your share of expenses, probably the cheapest option. But I have almost zero sailing experience, I'm a mediocre cook, and I wasn't confident about how I'd handle being stuck in 20 square metres with the same five people for three to four weeks. So, after an introductory weekend course on the North Sea with the Brussels Royal Yacht Club, during which I was seasick for an entire day, I decided I needed something more manageable. I joined the Morgenster as a "trainee", their term for a paying passenger who helps a little with sailing manoeuvres.

## The Morgenster

<img src="/adventures/atlantic-2025/thumb/morgenster.webp" alt="The Morgenster tall ship" class="inline-image float-left" />

The Morgenster (Dutch for "morning star") is a tall ship: a traditionally rigged sailing vessel with two or more masts. At 48 metres long with a main mast nearly 30 metres tall, she carries a crew of six to eight plus 12 to 20 trainees. The rhythm of ship life is structured around watches, meals, and sail manoeuvres, with plenty of open time in between.

## Tenerife

<img src="/adventures/atlantic-2025/thumb/tenerife_inline.webp" alt="Tenerife" class="inline-image float-right" />

I made my way to Tenerife by bus, train, and ferry, and had three days on the island before the ship departed. I had been before and knew it would be good. What makes Tenerife special is the variety: depending on altitude and which side of the island you're on, the landscape and climate change completely, from arid volcanic desert to lush cloud forest. The days on land felt precious. I knew they'd be my last for a while.

## The first week

<img src="/adventures/atlantic-2025/thumb/twilight.webp" alt="Twilight at sea" class="inline-image float-left" />

The first leg was Tenerife to Cabo Verde, roughly a week of sailing. Getting into the rhythm of ship life takes time; the watch schedule wrecked my sleep cycle for the first several days. I did 12–16h and 0–4h shifts that week, and spent my free time reading, practising ukulele, trying to photograph flying fish (they jump randomly, are tiny and fast, and are only out of the water for a few seconds), and chatting with crew and other trainees.

One of the highlights of the whole trip was climbing the main mast. It is nearly 30 metres tall and the ship is rolling the whole time, so you're swinging with it near the top. The scary part is the safety hook: each time you move it to a new anchor point on the way up, you are briefly free soloing, 20 or 25 metres above the deck. Exhilarating.

Every now and then I'd look up and see the same endless ocean, changing slowly as the day passed. It is endlessly beautiful, but beautiful like the sky rather than like a landscape passing through a train window. It is not entertainment, unless a group of dolphins shows up to play, which they did a couple of times.

## Cabo Verde

<img src="/adventures/atlantic-2025/thumb/caboverdehike1.webp" alt="Hiking in Cabo Verde" class="inline-image float-right" />

We stopped in Cabo Verde for three days. The north island, Santo Antão, was the highlight: beautiful, dramatic, and very poor. I did a couple of hikes with other passengers and crew, exploring in a pleasantly touristy way. It was also just good to be on solid land again for a bit.

## Crossing the Atlantic

<img src="/adventures/atlantic-2025/thumb/atthehelm.webp" alt="At the helm" class="inline-image float-left" />

The second leg, Cabo Verde to Fernando de Noronha, took about two weeks. I must have eaten something bad in Cabo Verde, because the first few days after we left I was sick: puking a few times and barely able to eat anything. Luckily the seas on that stretch were fairly calm, because the combination of illness and tropical sun was truly awful. This is where the psychological side of sailing really sets in. The ship rolls for days on end in suffocating tropical heat, you are always around the same people with nowhere to escape for privacy, no internet, no phone network, and only the rhythm of monotonous ship life.

<img src="/adventures/atlantic-2025/thumb/fous2.webp" alt="Boobies at sea" class="inline-image float-right" />

We had several days running on the engine through the doldrums (the belt of calm winds around the equator), which is both noisy and, from a climate perspective, not ideal. We also crossed the equator, marked by the traditional ceremony, and stopped in the middle of the ocean to swim, with 4000 metres of water below your feet.

<img src="/adventures/atlantic-2025/thumb/gold.webp" alt="Golden sunset over the Atlantic" class="inline-image" style="width:100%;margin:1.2em 0;" />

For me, the hardest part was the lack of personal space. On the last few days I often slept outside in the rescue dinghy, partly to escape the heat but mostly because it was the only spot on the ship that felt genuinely private.

The cook was a big part of what made daily life good: the food was tasty, she had a lot of personality, and she was something of the ship's mascot. I also won't forget many of the other characters onboard, crew and trainees alike: friendly, funny, weird, cool. The young crew members especially impressed me, some in their early twenties and already living an adventurous life at sea. I refrain from naming or showing portraits since I haven't asked permission.

## Fernando de Noronha

<img src="/adventures/atlantic-2025/thumb/noronha2.webp" alt="Fernando de Noronha" class="inline-image float-right" />

Our destination in Brazil was Fernando de Noronha, a small archipelago and marine national park about 350 km off the coast. I expected to stay a couple of days and move on, but I ended up staying a week.

<img src="/adventures/atlantic-2025/thumb/beach.webp" alt="Beach at Fernando de Noronha" class="inline-image float-left" />

I snorkelled every day from fine-sand beaches, encountering brightly coloured fish, sharks (scary-looking but inoffensive), and sea turtles. After weeks of rolling ocean and cramped quarters, lying in clear warm water watching a turtle drift past felt like a very good reward.
