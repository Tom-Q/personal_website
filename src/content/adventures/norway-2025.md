This was my second remote expedition after Patagonia. Like the first, it started with hours spent poring over satellite images, followed by months of planning, logistics, and physical preparation. On August 27, 2025, I found myself on the bank of a remote Norwegian reservoir, inflating my packraft, ready for another foray into the wild.

## Access

I flew from Amsterdam to Oslo, then took trains and buses north to limit my CO2 footprint. Far from perfect, but better than flying all the way to Bodø. Two days of quiet travel, ending with a taxi ride to the reservoir that marked the start of my route.

## Day by day

<img src="/adventures/norway-2025/thumb/iceberg.webp" class="float-right" alt="An iceberg stranded on the reservoir shore" />

**Day 1.** I paddled across the reservoir, passing a stranded iceberg on the way, an early reminder of how much glacial ice still shapes this landscape. I made camp on the far bank.

<img src="/adventures/norway-2025/thumb/lostvalley2.webp" class="float-left" alt="The majestic valley on day 2" />

**Day 2.** A 20km hike through a vast, open valley. The terrain was far easier than the maps had suggested, which was a relief. By evening I arrived at a glacial lake of an almost unreal blue, its colour drawn from mineral particles in the meltwater. The surface was completely still, mirroring the surrounding peaks.

<video src="/adventures/norway-2025/norway_web.mp4" controls playsinline width="100%"></video>

<img src="/adventures/norway-2025/thumb/bluelake2.webp" class="float-right" alt="The blue lake panorama" />

**Day 3.** I paddled across the blue lake and into the Glomåga rapids, class II/II+. It was my first time running real whitewater in an expedition context, and I found the rhythm of it satisfying: scout from shore, get back in, read the current, get shaken around a little.

<img src="/adventures/norway-2025/thumb/hut.webp" class="float-left" alt="The remote hut" />

As the river volume grew and the rapids increased in intensity, I pulled out for good. That's when I stumbled on a small, immaculate hut (30 visitors a year, according to the guest book). It changed the shape of the trip entirely: proper rest, a dry night, and space to consider an extension I hadn't planned.

<img src="/adventures/norway-2025/thumb/glaciertraverse.webp" class="float-right" alt="Crossing the glacier" />

**Day 4.** I went up the slopes from the hut to reach real alpine terrain - all rock and ice. I crossed a glacier to reach a natural balcony overlooking a completely isolated fjord. The glacier was the crux of the whole trip: I had opted for microspikes over crampons to save weight, and the exit was genuinely tricky.

<img src="/adventures/norway-2025/thumb/fjord3.webp" class="float-left" alt="The isolated fjord" />

**Day 5.** Hiked back to the hut by a slightly different route, avoiding the glacier traverse. I enjoyed another quiet recovery evening indoors, reading *The Hobbit* by candlelight.

<img src="/adventures/norway-2025/thumb/canyon2.webp" class="float-right" alt="The narrow canyon" />

**Day 6.** After a few kilometres on an actual trail, I briefly encountered a local family out for a day hike — the first people I'd seen since the taxi driver five days earlier. Then I cut across a small lake and into a narrow canyon leading to Austerdalsvatnet. Apart from the glacier exit, it was the hardest terrain of the trip, and it was sustained: 8km of struggling through that canyon, with occasional climbing moves on slippery rocks.

<img src="/adventures/norway-2025/thumb/river.webp" class="float-left" alt="Descending the Svartisåga" />

**Day 7.** I crossed Austerdalsvatnet and re-entered the world. Paddling down the Svartisåga toward Langvatnet, I came across two golden eagles feeding on a moose carcass — and then, minutes later, a live moose crossed the river directly in front of me.

**Day 8.** A final paddle across Langvatnet in pouring rain, and I arrived in Mo i Rana.

## Impressions

Everything went better than planned. I completed the full route and added two unplanned days up to the fjord balcony and back. The nature was relentless in its beauty: glaciers, fjords, canyons, blue lakes, eagles. Remote travel in Norway turned out to be remarkably approachable compared to Patagonia: the terrain is gentler and the logistics simpler. I also got lucky with the weather, which helped enormously. That said, it was still a genuine wilderness with no trails, no people, and no margin for serious mistakes. I'm already thinking about where to go next.
